# Chemistry Lab C — Event Breakdown

## Overview
- **Team size:** Up to 2 participants
- **Duration:** ~50 minutes
- **Format:** Lab/hands-on event combining tasks and questions
- **Eye Protection:** Category C required (goggles)
- **Calculator:** Class III — each participant may bring one

## Content Scope
The event focuses on two topic areas, and **at least 2 activities are required, with at least one covering each topic**:

### 1. Chemical Reactions / Stoichiometry
- Nomenclature and formula writing — participants must know symbols/charges for: nitrate, carbonate, phosphate, acetate, sulfate, ammonium, bicarbonate, hydroxide
- The "-ite" vs. "-ate" anion naming convention (one less oxygen = "-ite")
- Using a periodic table to determine charges of monatomic ions (e.g., Na⁺, S²⁻)
- Mole conversions and percentage yield calculations
- Classification of reaction types
- Balancing chemical equations
- Reaction prediction: products of metathesis (double displacement) reactions, solubility rules, oxidation-reduction reactions, writing total ionic and net ionic equations

### 2. Kinetics
- Measuring reaction rates and explaining how/why reaction conditions affect rate: **temperature, concentration, particle size, and catalysts**
- **At State & National levels only:** determining rate laws from actual experimentation or provided data, and determining rate constants **with correct units**

## Event Parameters
- Each participant brings: safety equipment (goggles, lab coat or apron), a writing implement, optionally a Class III calculator
- Each participant may bring **one double-sided 8.5"x11" sheet** of notes (can be laminated/sheet-protected)
- Teams should bring their own equipment from the **Division C Chemistry Events Lab Equipment List** (posted separately by Science Olympiad) — the Event Supervisor does NOT provide this equipment, so a team without it is genuinely disadvantaged
- Safety dress code is strict: goggles + apron/lab coat, skin covered neck-to-wrist-to-toes, loose-fitting pants, hair tied back if shoulder-length or longer. Violating this (or unsafe material handling) results in penalties or disqualification
- The Event Supervisor provides: any required reagents, extra glassware, and reference materials needed for the specific tasks (e.g., periodic table, standard reduction potential table, constants)

## What Actually Happens on Competition Day

1. **Check-in / safety check:** Team arrives dressed per the safety requirements (goggles, apron/lab coat, covered skin, tied-back hair) and with their own lab equipment from the approved list. The Event Supervisor checks for compliance — non-compliant teams may be penalized or disqualified, and improperly-equipped teams are simply at a disadvantage since nothing is provided for them.

2. **Task structure:** The competition consists of a series of tasks — this could be:
   - Hands-on activities (physically running a reaction, titration-style measurement, etc.)
   - Written questions on the listed topics
   - Interpreting provided data (graphs, diagrams, tables)
   - Observing an experiment the supervisor has already set up and running (rather than doing it themselves)
   - **At least 2 activities total, with at least one on chemical reactions/stoichiometry and at least one on kinetics.**

3. **Possible probeware task:** Teams may be shown a demonstration of a sensor/probe setup by the supervisor, then be given a resulting dataset to interpret and analyze — they are not necessarily operating the probe themselves.

4. **Working through content:** Depending on the specific tasks assigned, participants might:
   - Be given an unknown reaction and asked to classify its type, balance it, and predict products (including writing net ionic equations if it's a precipitation/metathesis reaction)
   - Do stoichiometry calculations — mole conversions, percent yield — possibly tied to a hands-on measurement they just performed
   - Be given a kinetics dataset (e.g., concentration vs. time, or rate at varying temperatures) and asked to explain the trend, or (State/Nationals) actually derive the rate law and calculate the rate constant with proper units
   - Answer conceptual questions about *why* a variable (temp, concentration, particle size, catalyst) affects rate, tied to collision theory

5. **Time management:** Time may be limited at individual tasks/stations, but per the rules, **time is explicitly NOT used as a scoring factor or tiebreaker** — so there's no bonus for finishing fast, but running out of time on a task still means missing those points.

6. **Cleanup:** At the end, teams are expected to leave their area clean — failing to do so incurs a real point penalty.

## Scoring
- **High score wins.**
- **Points are split evenly between the two topic areas** (kinetics gets equal weight to chemical reactions/stoichiometry overall)
- **Tiebreakers:** pre-selected questions chosen in advance by the Event Supervisor
- **Penalties:**
  - Up to **10% penalty** if the work area isn't cleaned up as instructed
  - Up to **10% penalty** if a team brings prohibited lab equipment to the event

## Key Competitive Realities
- **This event punishes being under-equipped just as much as being under-prepared academically** — since the Event Supervisor does not provide standard lab equipment, teams that don't bring gear from the official equipment list are at a real, structural disadvantage regardless of their chemistry knowledge.
- **Ion nomenclature and charges are foundational** and appear constantly across both stoichiometry and reaction-prediction tasks — this is testable, memorizable content that pays off disproportionately given how often it's a prerequisite for other questions.
- **Kinetics has a hard content cliff at State/Nationals** — Regional-level competitors only need conceptual understanding of what affects rate, while State/National competitors must be able to actually derive a rate law and rate constant from raw data, including correctly identifying units (which vary depending on the overall reaction order) — this is a meaningfully harder quantitative skill that Regional-only prep won't cover.
- **Safety compliance is a real, non-academic point risk** — dress code violations or unsafe handling can result in penalties or disqualification independent of chemistry knowledge, so this needs to be treated as seriously as the content itself.
- **The one-page cheat sheet is likely optimized around formulas/charges/reference data** (ion charges, common reaction types, rate law formulas, unit conversions) rather than conceptual explanations, since conceptual reasoning has to happen live.
