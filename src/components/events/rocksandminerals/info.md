# Rocks and Minerals C — Event Breakdown

## Overview
- **Team size:** Up to 2 participants
- **Duration:** ~50 minutes
- **Format:** Task-oriented identification and analysis event, run as stations and/or written questions
- **Calculator:** Class II — each team may bring one

## Event Parameters
- Each team may bring **one magnifying glass** and **one three-ring binder of any size** containing information in any form from any source, attached via the rings. Sheet protectors, lamination, tabs, and labels are all permitted.
- Each team may also bring **one commercially produced field guide**, which may be tabbed and annotated.
- In addition to the binder and field guide, each team may bring **one copy of the 2026 National Rocks and Minerals List**, which does NOT need to be secured in the binder and may be annotated separately.
- Teams may bring **one Class II calculator**.
- Teams are **not permitted to bring their own samples or specimens**.
- If the event runs as a station rotation with samples/specimens/displays, **no material may be removed from the binder** during the event — **except** the National Rocks and Minerals List, which can be freely referenced throughout.

## Content Scope
Identification is limited to specimens on the **Official Science Olympiad 2026 National Rocks and Minerals List**, though other (non-listed) rocks/minerals may be used purely to illustrate concepts (not for ID). Key structural rules:
- **Identification makes up 30–50% of available points** — the rest is conceptual/process-based
- **Questions/tasks are balanced between rock topics and mineral topics**
- Written descriptions of how a specimen *might* react to hydrochloric acid (HCl) may be given, but HCl itself is never used/provided, and **taste testing is never allowed**
- If a specimen can't be identified purely by observation, key diagnostic properties will be given in the question itself

### Mineral Topics
- **Identification** using observable properties (or given diagnostic characteristics when observation isn't enough)
- **Physical properties:** color, hardness, luster, streak, cleavage/fracture, density/specific gravity/heft, diaphaneity, tenacity
- **Other properties:** reaction with acid, fluorescence, magnetism, smell, taste (described only, never tested), double refraction, piezoelectricity, radioactivity
- **Mineral habit:** acicular, bladed, botryoidal, cubic, dendritic, dodecahedral, doubly terminated, druzy, geodic, hexagonal, hopper, massive, micaceous, octahedral, pisolitic, prismatic, radiating, rosette, stalactitic, twinning, tabular
- **Chemical composition:** formulas, chemistry-property relationships (e.g., trace elements affecting color); solid solution series (feldspar ternary diagrams); phase diagram interpretation (temp/pressure, temp/composition, limited to two-component systems)
- **Polymorphs:** e.g., aragonite/calcite, diamond/graphite, orthoclase/microcline, sillimanite/andalusite/kyanite
- **Classification:** mineral families/groups by composition (feldspar, garnet, tourmaline groups); silicate tetrahedra structures (nesosilicates, sorosilicates, inosilicates, cyclosilicates, phyllosilicates, tectosilicates)
  - **State/National only:** Crystal systems (cubic, tetragonal, orthorhombic, monoclinic, triclinic, trigonal, hexagonal) and how crystal structure relates to physical properties like cleavage planes/crystal shape
- **Formation methods/environments:** hydrothermal, chemical weathering, crystallization from magma, evaporites, chemical precipitation, heat/pressure alteration
- **Minerals tied to rock-forming environments:** evaporite minerals in sedimentary settings, mafic minerals in oceanic crust, metamorphic-condition minerals
- **Bowen's Reaction Series:** relationship between mineral crystallization order and magma temperature
- **Uses of minerals:** ores, industry, jewelry, geochronology, medicine, manufacturing, construction, electronics; precious/semi-precious gemstone varieties (from the list, plus emerald, aquamarine, morganite, peridot, ruby, sapphire, pearl, amber)

### Rock Topics
- **Identification** using observable characteristics (or provided diagnostic info, e.g., mineral composition of fine-grained igneous rocks, or acid reaction of chalk/diatomite)
- **Classification:** igneous, sedimentary, metamorphic — via diagnostic features (glassy/vesicular = igneous; rounded grains/fossils/layers = sedimentary; foliation/banding = metamorphic)
- **Igneous rocks:**
  - Textures: aphanitic, glassy, vesicular, porphyritic, pyroclastic, phaneritic, pegmatitic
  - Composition: felsic, intermediate, mafic, ultramafic
  - Intrusive/extrusive environments and formations: batholith, dike, sill, volcanic neck, lava flow, pyroclastic flow, laccolith
  - Relationship between texture and formation environment (solidification rate)
  - Igneous rock/mineral types tied to plate boundary type (basalt at mid-ocean ridges, andesite/diorite at subduction zones, granite at convergent boundaries)
- **Sedimentary rocks:**
  - Textures: clastic, chemical (crystalline), biochemical (organic/bioclastic)
  - Grain size, sorting, and shape (round vs. angular) and what they imply about depositional energy/conditions
  - Relationship between texture/composition and depositional environment
  - Environments of deposition: alluvial fan, delta, river/stream, lake, swamp, wind, floodplain, beach, shallow marine/shelf, deep marine
  - Primary sedimentary structures and what they imply: plane bedding, crossbedding, ripple marks, mud cracks, graded bedding, fossil tracks/trails
- **Metamorphic rocks:**
  - Textures: foliated (slaty cleavage, schistose, banding) vs. non-foliated
  - Mineral composition, including index minerals that form from heat/pressure (garnet, corundum, kyanite, staurolite, epidote, andalusite, sillimanite, chlorite)
  - Protoliths (parent rocks): e.g., shale→slate, limestone→marble, sandstone→quartzite
  - Regional vs. contact metamorphism
  - Grade of metamorphism (low/medium/high) and which index minerals indicate which grade
  - Relationship of temperature/pressure/depth to metamorphic facies (hornfels, zeolite, greenschist, amphibolite, granulite, blueschist, eclogite) via graph/chart interpretation
  - Metamorphic environments in a plate tectonics context (mountain building at continental-continental convergence; blueschist/eclogite at subduction zones; greenschist from basalt/gabbro at divergent boundaries)
- **Rock cycle:** the geologic processes that interconvert rock types (melting/solidification; uplift/erosion/deposition; burial/compaction/cementation; heat/pressure → recrystallization/deformation)
- **Economic importance/uses:** building stone, ores, ornamental use, agriculture, fossil fuels
- **State/National only — Thin sections:** using photomicrographs (photos taken through a microscope in polarized light) to:
  - Identify minerals via optical properties (twinning, extinction, cleavage planes, birefringence) — limited to microcline, plagioclase, calcite, augite, garnet
  - Distinguish igneous vs. sedimentary rock types by microscopic texture (e.g., basalt vs. pumice; oolites, sandstone vs. arkose)

## The Official List (structure, not full contents)
The 2026 National Rocks and Minerals List separates minerals and rocks into their own sections, with asterisked (*) items marked as **State/National only**. Some list footnotes matter for identification purposes:
- For ID purposes, diagnostic info (chemical formula, grain size, composition) will be provided for certain items when needed
- The pink/tan feldspar variety is identified as "Orthoclase/Microcline" or "Potassium feldspar"
- Tourmaline is accepted as a generic ID even though it's technically a mineral group
- Bauxite is classified as a **sedimentary rock** (not its older classification)
- Rock Salt/Rock Gypsum are treated the same as the minerals Halite/Gypsum for ID purposes — no need to distinguish rock vs. mineral form

## What Actually Happens on Competition Day

1. **Check-in:** Team arrives with their magnifying glass, one binder (any size), one field guide, one copy of the National Rocks and Minerals List, and one Class II calculator. No specimens of their own are allowed.

2. **Format:** The Event Supervisor decides whether this runs as a station rotation, a straight written test, or some combination.

3. **Station rotation (if used):** Participants move station to station with **predetermined, announced time limits** per station. They may NOT go back to a previous station once they've moved on, but they CAN still update/add to their answers for stations they're currently working through the mechanism the supervisor sets up — essentially, once you leave a station, that station's answers are locked.

4. **Task types encountered:**
   - Direct specimen identification (observing a rock or mineral sample or photo and identifying it by name)
   - Diagnostic property interpretation when full observation isn't possible (given hardness, streak, or other data instead of a hands-on sample)
   - Graph/chart interpretation (e.g., phase diagrams, metamorphic facies charts, ternary feldspar diagrams)
   - Photomicrograph interpretation at State/Nationals (identifying minerals or distinguishing rock textures from microscope images in polarized light)
   - Written conceptual questions about formation processes, plate tectonic context, or economic uses

5. **Working through the event:** Because points are split 30-50% identification and the rest process/concept-based, and because rock/mineral questions are explicitly balanced, teams need genuine competency in both halves — pure ID drilling alone caps out well below full credit.

6. **Submission:** Team submits written responses (station sheets or exam packet) at the end.

## Scoring
- **High score wins.**
- Point values assigned per question/section (may vary; not necessarily uniform)
- **Tiebreakers:** preselected questions/sections

## Key Competitive Realities
- **This event is explicitly NOT primarily identification** — with ID capped at 30-50% of points and the rest being conceptual (formation processes, plate tectonic context, classification systems, diagrams), a team that only drills flashcard-style specimen ID will leave a large fraction of available points on the table.
- **The list is closed and fully specified** — unlike some events, exact content (which minerals, which rocks, which varieties) is fully known in advance via the official list, making comprehensive, exhaustive coverage of the list genuinely achievable rather than a guessing game.
- **Diagnostic-property reasoning is a distinct skill from pure visual ID** — since the rules explicitly allow supervisors to give diagnostic data instead of requiring direct observation (e.g., "this fine-grained igneous rock has X mineral composition"), teams need to be able to identify specimens from *described* properties, not just from looking at a physical sample.
- **Graph/diagram interpretation is heavily featured and often under-prepared** — phase diagrams, ternary diagrams, and metamorphic facies charts require genuine quantitative/spatial reasoning skill, not memorization, and are explicitly called out multiple times in the rules.
- **State/Nationals adds an entirely new skill: reading thin-section photomicrographs** — optical mineralogy in polarized light (recognizing twinning, extinction, birefringence) is a specialized skill totally absent from Regional-level competition, requiring dedicated new prep rather than an extension of existing memorization.
- **The rock-cycle and plate-tectonics-context questions connect rock/mineral facts to bigger-picture geologic processes** — meaning strong prep should connect "what is this rock" to "how did it form and in what tectonic setting," not treat identification and process knowledge as separate silos.
- **Footnote rules matter and are easy to miss** — e.g., Bauxite's reclassification as sedimentary, or the Rock Salt/Halite equivalence — these are exactly the kind of small, easy-to-overlook rules that cost points if not specifically reviewed.
