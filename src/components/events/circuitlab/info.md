# Circuit Lab C — Event Breakdown

## Overview
- **Team size:** Up to 2 participants
- **Duration:** ~50 minutes
- **Format:** Two-part event — Part I is a written test, Part II is hands-on tasks
- **Calculator:** Class III — each team may bring two

## Event Parameters
- Each team may bring a collection of notes/resources (any size binder, notebooks, folders, sheet protectors, lamination, tabs, labels all permitted), containing information in any form from any source — this is **much more permissive** than the single-sheet limit seen in many other written-test events
- Each team may bring writing utensils and two Class III calculators
- The Event Supervisor provides all supplies and measurement devices needed for the hands-on tasks
- Participants may bring their **own basic multimeters** to use instead of supervisor-provided ones, at the supervisor's discretion

## Content Scope

### Part I: Written Test
Covers electricity and magnetism knowledge. The test will include **at least one question from each** of these areas:
- Properties of electric charges/fields, sources/hazards of static electricity, Coulomb's Law, capacitance
- Direct current (DC) characteristics, sources, uses, hazards
- Alternating current (AC) characteristics, sources, uses, hazards, common household AC components
- Concepts and units of current, voltage, resistance, power, energy, and applying Ohm's Law
- Magnetic poles/fields, electromagnets, transformers, motors/generators, the right-hand rule
- Electrical control devices: switches, relays, fuses, ground fault circuit interrupters (GFCIs), breakers
- Simple circuit calculations, constructions, and configurations, including reading/drawing simple circuit diagrams
- Simple circuit analysis using Kirchhoff's Voltage and Current Laws
- Historical figures and their contributions: Ampere, Coulomb, Kirchhoff, Volta, Ohm, Tesla, Faraday
- **State/Nationals only:** LED fundamentals, basic silicon PN junction characteristics (diodes, PNP, NPN transistors), basic op-amp principles and applications

**Explicitly excluded from the event at any level:** semiconductors beyond what's listed above, AC circuit theory, frequency analysis, inductance, any calculus/differential-equation-based calculations, non-linear devices, 3-phase power, and oscilloscopes.

Unless a question says otherwise, answers must be given in metric units with appropriate significant figures.

### Part II: Hands-On Tasks
At least one hands-on task/station is included. Participants must be comfortable using breadboards and multimeters, and may ask the Event Supervisor about the internal wiring of any breadboards used. Example task types explicitly given in the rules (not exhaustive):
- Determine the value of a mystery resistor in a circuit using **only voltage measurements** (no direct resistance measurement)
- Calculate the power supplied to a circuit
- Given wires, batteries, resistors, and 2 LEDs, wire them so the LEDs are **equally bright**
- Construct an electromagnet using wire, a bolt, and a battery
- Given a USB charger, read its label, determine its various output power levels, and calculate charging time for a specific battery

## What Actually Happens on Competition Day

1. **Check-in:** Team arrives with their notes/binder (no size or format restriction beyond being paper-based), writing utensils, and up to 2 Class III calculators. They may optionally bring their own multimeter.

2. **Part I — Written Test:** The team works together through a written test covering the electricity/magnetism topics above. Since at least one question is guaranteed from each listed subtopic, the test is broad rather than deep in any single area (except at State/Nationals, where the added semiconductor/op-amp content raises the ceiling). Historical-figure questions (who discovered/is credited with what) are a small but *guaranteed* scoring category worth quick review.

3. **Part II — Hands-On Tasks:** The team moves to a hands-on station (or stations) where the Event Supervisor has already set up the required supplies (breadboards, resistors, wires, batteries, LEDs, etc. — participants don't bring these). Tasks require them to physically build or measure something — e.g., wiring a circuit correctly, using a multimeter to take specific readings, or performing a calculation based on a physical measurement they just took (like reading power output off a USB charger's label and doing a battery-charging-time calculation).

4. **Working within task constraints:** For tasks like the "mystery resistor" example, the constraint itself is the challenge — determining resistance **without directly measuring resistance** requires applying Ohm's Law creatively using only voltage readings. For the "equally bright LEDs" task, this tests practical understanding of how current/voltage division works in series vs. parallel branches.

5. **Multimeter competency:** Since participants must be "familiar with the operation of breadboards and multimeters," a meaningful part of hands-on scoring depends on knowing *how* to correctly measure voltage (parallel connection) vs. current (series connection, breaking the circuit) — a common source of real point loss if done incorrectly.

## Scoring
- **High score wins.**
- Points awarded for correct answers, measurements, calculations, and data analysis
- **Written portion = 50-75% of total score**
- **Hands-on portion = remaining 25-50% of total score**
- (Exact split within those ranges is at the Event Supervisor's discretion, not fixed)
- **Tiebreakers:** pre-selected task(s)/question(s), which may be marked on the written test in advance

## Key Competitive Realities
- **This is one of the more resource-permissive written-test events** — since there's no single-sheet limit, teams can effectively bring a full reference binder, meaning the actual bottleneck is less "did we memorize this" and more "can we quickly locate/apply the right formula or concept under time pressure" and "do we understand circuit behavior well enough to answer conceptual/analytical questions the binder can't directly answer for us" (e.g., Kirchhoff's Law problems, mystery resistor logic).
- **The hands-on portion tests physical competency, not just theory** — a team that knows Ohm's Law perfectly but has never actually used a breadboard or multimeter in practice will likely underperform on Part II regardless of Part I score.
- **The excluded-topics list is worth knowing explicitly** — since oscilloscopes, AC circuit theory, inductance, and calculus-based analysis are all explicitly out of scope, prep time is better spent going deep on the listed topics (especially Kirchhoff's Laws, which require genuine problem-solving skill) rather than broadening into excluded territory.
- **State/Nationals adds real new content** (LEDs, diodes/transistors, op-amps) that Regional-level prep won't cover, so teams advancing need a distinct study phase for this material rather than assuming their Regional-level prep transfers directly.
- **The historical figures question is a "free" guaranteed point** if studied — a short, discrete list (7 names) that's easy to prep for relative to its guaranteed appearance on the test.
