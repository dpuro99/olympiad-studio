# Designer Genes C — Event Breakdown

## Overview
- **Team size:** Up to 2 participants
- **Duration:** ~50 minutes
- **Format:** Written test, which may be arranged in stations
- **Calculator:** Class II — each team may bring two
- **Allowed resource:** One double-sided 8.5"x11" sheet of notes (may be laminated/sheet-protected; no affixed labels, no multiple sheets)

## Content Philosophy
The rules explicitly state that questions **emphasize process skills**: quantitative reasoning, making calculations, analyzing and interpreting experimental results, and drawing evidence-based conclusions — not simple recall. The topic list is described as "exhaustive" (i.e., nothing outside this list should appear), covering classic, evolutionary, and molecular genetics without over-weighting any single topic.

## Content Scope

### 1. Mendelian Genetics
- Mendel's Laws of Inheritance and exceptions/violations (linkage, incomplete dominance, codominance, complementation)
- Constructing and using Punnett Squares for mono-, di-, and trihybrid crosses
- Predicting genotype/phenotype probabilities using Punnett Squares and probability rules on experimental data
- Evaluating and constructing pedigrees (dominant vs. recessive, autosomal vs. sex-linked)
- Epistasis and its effect on predicted outcomes
- **State/National only:** Mapping 2 (State) or 3 (National) genes on a chromosome using recombination frequency data

### 2. Mitosis and Meiosis
- Comparing/contrasting major stages and structures of mitosis vs. meiosis
- Effects of nondisjunction; using karyotype data to detect chromosomal abnormalities
- **State/National only:** Somatic recombination in immune cells — V(D)J recombination and immunoglobulin class switching

### 3. Population & Evolutionary Genetics
- Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium: assumptions, calculations, and consequences of violating those assumptions
- Genetic drift, founder effects, bottlenecks, migration, relative fitness — how these relate to changing allele frequencies
- How additive alleles create continuous variation; estimating the number of genes contributing to a trait
- Gene duplication's role in genome/new gene evolution; identifying homologs, orthologs, paralogs
- Phylogenetic trees: interpreting them for nuclear/organellar/extrachromosomal elements; basic steps of building one from sequence data
- **State/National only:** Heritability — calculating broad-sense, narrow-sense, and realized heritability

### 4. Molecular Biology of DNA
- DNA component structure (nucleotides, backbone), directionality (5'/3' ends), base pairing rules
- Stages of DNA replication (pre-replication complex through termination), and detailed replication fork processes
- How DNA polymerases achieve replication fidelity and proofreading mechanisms
- DNA structural organization: plasmids, chromatin complexes, euchromatin vs. heterochromatin, chromosomes
- DNA damage processes and repair mechanisms
- Classifying DNA mutations (chromosomal rearrangements, insertions, deletions, substitutions) and their protein-level impact (frameshift, silent, missense, nonsense)

### 5. Prokaryotic Gene Expression & Regulation
- Central Dogma of Molecular Biology and reverse transcription
- Transcription stages (initiation, elongation, termination) and RNA polymerase mechanism
- Cis- and trans-regulatory elements (promoters, enhancers, silencers, riboswitches, transcription factors) in context of a gene, and specifically the *lac* and *trp* operons
- Translation stages, ribosome mechanism, mRNA/tRNA roles, and how translation is regulated
- **State/National only:** Protein secretion systems (Sec and Tat systems)

### 6. Technology and Techniques
- PCR: denaturation/annealing/extension steps, role of temperature, required components, what experimental questions PCR can answer
- Sanger sequencing: how it differs from standard PCR, what experimental questions it can answer
- Next-gen (Illumina) vs. third-gen (Nanopore) sequencing: platforms, how each works, data produced, and questions each can address
- Molecular cloning: major steps to clone a gene into an organism; utility of plasmids, restriction enzymes, PCR, Gibson assembly, blue-white screening
- Knockout/knockdown/complementation experiments: how they determine gene function, and why one approach might be chosen over another
  - **State/National only:** Specific techniques — RNAi, homologous recombination, CRISPR, TALENs
- **State/National only:** Basic procedure, applications, and limitations of ChIP-seq, Hi-C, and RNA-Seq

## What Actually Happens on Competition Day

1. **Check-in:** Team arrives with writing utensils, up to 2 Class II calculators, and their single double-sided cheat sheet.

2. **Test format:** The written test may be delivered as a single packet or broken into stations — either way, it's a written-answer format (not a hands-on lab format like Chemistry Lab or Forensics).

3. **Question types encountered:** Given the "process skills" emphasis, questions will frequently require the team to:
   - Perform a Punnett Square cross and calculate resulting genotype/phenotype ratios or probabilities
   - Read a pedigree and determine the most likely inheritance pattern (or construct one from a case study description)
   - Run a Hardy-Weinberg calculation given allele or genotype frequency data
   - Interpret a phylogenetic tree or determine relationships between sequences
   - Work through a DNA replication or mutation scenario and predict the downstream protein-level consequence
   - Interpret an experimental technique's output (e.g., what a given PCR/sequencing setup would and wouldn't be able to answer) rather than just define the technique
   - At State/Nationals: perform gene-mapping calculations from recombination frequency data, or calculate heritability values

4. **Working through the test:** Because so many question types are calculation- or reasoning-based rather than pure recall, the calculator allowance (Class II) is functionally important — teams will be doing real math (probability calculations, Hardy-Weinberg algebra, recombination frequency percentages) under time pressure.

5. **Submission:** Team turns in their completed test/station answers at the end of the period.

## Scoring
- **High score wins.**
- Points awarded for **quality and accuracy of answers, quality of supporting reasoning, and use of proper scientific methods** — meaning partial credit and reasoning-based grading are explicitly part of the rubric, not just right/wrong answers
- **Tiebreakers:** pre-selected questions

## Key Competitive Realities
- **This event rewards applied problem-solving over memorization far more than most Science Olympiad life-science events** — the rules explicitly flag "process skills," and grading explicitly credits "quality of supporting reasoning," so teams that can only recite definitions will underperform relative to teams that can actually execute a Hardy-Weinberg calculation or interpret a novel phylogenetic tree they've never seen before.
- **The topic list spans classic, molecular, and evolutionary genetics simultaneously** — this is a broad content area, and since the rules state the topic list is "exhaustive" (nothing tested outside it) but also that "no overemphasis on any one particular topic" is used, prep needs to cover all six major sections roughly evenly rather than betting heavily on one area.
- **State/Nationals adds a meaningful jump in quantitative difficulty** — three-point gene mapping (vs. two-point at State), heritability calculations, and more advanced sequencing technique questions (ChIP-seq, Hi-C, RNA-Seq) all require material and skills beyond Regional-level prep.
- **Technique-based questions test application, not just definitions** — e.g., knowing "what PCR does" isn't enough; questions may ask what experimental question a given technique *can or can't* answer, requiring genuine understanding of each method's capabilities and limitations.
- **The cheat sheet is likely most valuable as a formula/process reference** (Hardy-Weinberg equation, Punnett square setup shortcuts, key operon diagrams, technique comparison tables) rather than a place to cram raw factual recall, since the test rewards applying concepts to novel scenarios more than restating memorized facts.
