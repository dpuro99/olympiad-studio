# Entomology C — Event Breakdown

## Overview
- **Team size:** Up to 2 participants
- **Duration:** ~50 minutes
- **Format:** Identification-based written/station event (specimens/images shown, questions asked about each)

## Event Parameters
- Each team may bring **one three-ring binder, 2" or smaller** (measured by interior ring diameter), containing information in any form from any source. Sheet protectors, lamination, tabs, and labels are all permitted.
- Each team may also bring **one commercially produced field guide**, which may be tabbed and annotated.
- In addition to the binder and field guide, each team may bring **one copy of the 2026 National Entomology List** (or a State/Regional list if issued) — this does NOT have to be inside the binder and may be annotated separately.
- Each team may bring **one hand lens or magnifying glass**. The Event Supervisor provides dissecting microscopes if needed.
- Teams may **not** bring their own samples/specimens.
- If the event uses a rotation through stations with samples/specimens/displays, **no material may be removed from the binder** during the event — **except** the National Entomology List, which can be freely carried and referenced.

## Content Scope
Identification is limited to insects/organisms appearing on the **2026 National Entomology List** (based on the *Insects of North America Princeton Field Guide*, 2023), which contains:
- **26 orders, 2 subclasses, and 113 families**
- All specimens represent insects found in the **Contiguous United States**
- Items marked with an asterisk (*) require participants to also recognize **larval/nymph forms**, not just adults
- Items in *italics* on the official list are **State/National only** — not tested at Regionals
- Includes one non-insect arthropod group: ticks (Subclass Acari, Family Ixodidae)

For each specimen shown, participants may be asked correlated questions about:
- **Internal and external anatomy**
- **Ecology:** habitats, environmental adaptations, behavior, relationships with other organisms (symbiosis, competition), relationships with plants/animals/public health, climate change impacts
- **Economic characteristics:** beneficial or detrimental roles — as sources of food, medicine, disease, chemicals, nutrients, or as nuisance species
- **Systematics:** where an organism fits taxonomically and why
- **State/National only:** Management-related questions — pest/disease/invasive species concerns, Integrated Pest Management (IPM: combining chemical, biological, and cultural controls with economic/environmental/social consequences in mind), conservation, and urban entomology

## What Actually Happens on Competition Day

1. **Check-in:** Team arrives with their binder (≤2" ring diameter), one field guide, one copy of the National Entomology List, and one hand lens/magnifying glass.

2. **Specimen presentation:** The Event Supervisor exhibits insect specimens or images (including nymph/larva forms for the orders/families that require it) in a way that lets participants examine pertinent features with the naked eye or a hand lens.

3. **Identification tasks:** For each specimen or image, the team must identify it by whatever taxonomic level is specified — **Order, Subclass, Family, or common name** — this varies by question, so participants need familiarity with the taxonomy at multiple levels, not just one.

4. **Dichotomous key work:** Participants may be asked to **use a provided dichotomous key** to identify a specimen, OR to **formulate their own simple dichotomous key** from a set of specimens/characteristics — this is an explicit, distinct skill from pure memorized ID.

5. **Correlated questions:** After (or alongside) identification, the team answers additional questions tied to that specific specimen — covering anatomy, ecology, economic importance, or (at State/Nationals) management/pest-control topics.

6. **Working through the event:** Because the identification list has 113 families across 26 orders, and questions can hit any of the four content categories (anatomy/ecology/economic/systematics) for any given specimen, this event effectively combines two skills: **fast, accurate visual ID** and **deep factual knowledge about the organism once identified**.

7. **Submission:** Team turns in written answers at the end of the period (or after completing station rotations, if run that way).

## Scoring
- **High score wins.**
- **Tiebreakers:** preselected questions

## Key Competitive Realities
- **This is fundamentally a visual pattern-recognition event layered on top of a factual-recall event** — a team can know everything about a species' ecology and economic importance, but if they can't correctly identify the specimen from a picture or physical sample in the first place, they can't access those points. Both skills need dedicated, separate practice.
- **The larval/nymph requirement (marked with *) is a distinct, often under-prepared skill** — many students study adult insect identification thoroughly but neglect immature forms, even though the rules explicitly flag which orders/families require recognizing larvae/nymphs.
- **The dichotomous key skill is testable independent of memorized ID** — a team could theoretically identify an unfamiliar specimen correctly using a provided key even without having memorized it, or could be asked to construct a simple key themselves from a set of specimens — this is a genuinely different skill from flashcard-style memorization and needs its own practice.
- **113 families is a large but *closed* set** — unlike some events with open-ended content, the entomology list is fully specified in advance, meaning comprehensive coverage of the exact list is achievable and highly valuable (no guessing what might show up).
- **The National Entomology List itself can be freely referenced during the event** (unlike other binder material during station rotations) — meaning participants don't need to have taxonomic names 100% memorized, but they DO need to be practiced at quickly cross-referencing what they're looking at against the list and their binder/field guide under time pressure.
- **Economic/ecological "use" questions reward broader context knowledge** — e.g., knowing an insect's role as a pollinator, disease vector, agricultural pest, or beneficial predator — this is more "concept and context" knowledge than pure taxonomy, and is a distinct study area from ID drilling.
- **State/National adds a management/policy layer** (IPM, conservation, urban entomology) that Regional-level prep doesn't require — this is conceptually different from anatomy/ecology facts and closer to applied decision-making about pest control tradeoffs.
