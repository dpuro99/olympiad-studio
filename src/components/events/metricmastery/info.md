# Metric Mastery B — Event Breakdown

## Overview
- **Team size:** Up to 2 participants
- **Duration:** ~50 minutes
- **Format:** Three-part event — estimation stations, a metric conversion sprint, and measurement stations
- **Calculator:** Class II — teams may bring two, but **only for Section Three** (not allowed during Sections One or Two)

## Event Parameters
- The event has **3 distinct sections**, run in a fixed order: **Section One (Estimation) → Section Two (Metric Unit Conversion) → Section Three (Measurement)**
- Sections One and Three both involve rotating through stations examining the **same or identical objects** — Section One asks teams to estimate a property, Section Three asks them to actually measure it
- Some stations require **calculated measurements** (formula-based, e.g., density, surface area, velocity) rather than direct reads. The percentage of stations that are calculation-based scales with tournament level:
  - Regionals: no more than 20% of stations
  - State: 20–40% of stations
  - Nationals: 40–60% of stations
- **Measuring devices must stay out of sight during Section One** (estimation) — no peeking at rulers/scales before guessing
- Each station's directions specify the property to estimate/measure and the required unit
- **Participants may NOT bring:** watches, writing implements, electronic devices (except the Section Three calculator), notes, or any improvised measuring tool (fingers, paper, pencils, clothing, etc.)
- The Event Supervisor provides all writing implements, paper, and measuring devices
- **All units must be metric (SI)** — no imperial/standard units anywhere in the event

## What Actually Happens on Competition Day

### Section One: Estimation
1. Team rotates through a series of stations, each displaying an object (or the same object type across teams) with a specific property to estimate (mass, area, volume, density, force, distance, time, or temperature).
2. **Time limit per station** (minimum, scales tighter with tournament level):
   - Regionals: ≥60 seconds/station
   - State: ≥45 seconds/station
   - Nationals: ≥30 seconds/station
3. Participants **cannot touch or feel objects** unless station directions explicitly allow it — **except** they are always allowed to "heft" an object (pick it up briefly) specifically for mass estimation.
4. No measuring devices are visible or usable — this is pure sensory/experiential estimation.
5. Each answer is recorded on an answer sheet, which must be turned in before the team moves to the next section (failure to turn it in = losing the score for that whole section).

### Section Two: Metric Unit Conversion
1. Runs strictly **after Section One finishes and before Section Three begins** — it's a standalone timed sprint in the middle of the event.
2. Team has **5 minutes to complete 5 metric unit conversion problems**.
3. All conversions are **metric-to-metric only** (e.g., mm to hm) — never metric-to-standard/imperial.
4. At the Regional level, supervisors provide a **metric prefix chart** with all needed prefixes — this scaffolding is not guaranteed at State/Nationals (implied by the rule only specifying it for Regionals).

### Section Three: Measurement
1. Team rotates through stations again, this time measuring the same/identical objects using **supervisor-supplied instruments**.
2. **Time limit per station** (minimum, scales tighter with tournament level):
   - Regionals: ≥90 seconds/station
   - State: ≥75 seconds/station
   - Nationals: ≥60 seconds/station
3. **Critical rule on precision:** Measurements must be expressed to the instrument's resolution (the smallest marked division on its scale) **plus one estimated digit** if the instrument is analog. Digital instruments get **no** estimated digit added.
   - Example given in the rules: if the correct answer is "9.0 cm," writing "9" or even "9.0" without matching the required resolution/estimated-digit convention is marked **wrong** — this is a strict, specific formatting rule that trips up unprepared teams.
4. Some stations require **calculated measurements** — e.g., calculating area/volume of polygons/polyhedrons, density of liquids/solids/powders, melting/boiling points of water, force, mechanical energy, displacement of liquid by a solid, time constants/periods of simple harmonic motion, mass of solids/liquids, or spring constants — all using values the team measured themselves at that station, then computing a formula-based result.

## Scoring
- **High score wins. Final Score = Estimation Score + Metric Unit Conversion Score + Measurement Score**

### Section One (Estimation) Scoring
Points awarded based on how close the estimate is to the actual value, as a **percentage range** that gets tighter at higher tournament levels:

| Points | Regionals | State | Nationals |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 pts | within 15% | within 10% | within 5% |
| 3 pts | within 30% | within 20% | within 10% |
| 1 pt | within 45% | within 30% | within 15% |

### Section Two (Conversion) Scoring
- Each of the 5 conversions is worth 5 points **only if the unit is also correctly written** — otherwise it's worth 0 points (no partial credit for a correct number with wrong/missing units).

### Section Three (Measurement) Scoring
- **Direct (non-calculated) measurements — analog instruments:** scored by how many "estimated digit" increments off the true value the answer is, again tightening by level:

| Points | Regionals | State | Nationals |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 pts | ±15 of estimated digit | ±20 of estimated digit... | ... |
| 3 pts | ±12 | ±17 | ±10, ±15 |
| 1 pt | ±10 | ±15 | ±20 |

*(Note: the extracted table formatting is imperfect in the source document, but the core structure is: full credit for very close, partial credit for close, and a widening tolerance band the lower the tournament level.)*

- **Direct measurements — digital instruments (same scoring at all levels):**
  - 5 pts: ±1 final digit
  - 3 pts: ±2 final digits
  - 1 pt: ±3 final digits
- **Calculated measurements** (area, volume, density, etc.) use the **same tolerance-band logic** as direct analog measurements, but applied to the final calculated result — with a worked example in the rules showing how a small measurement error compounds through a multiplication (e.g., length × width for area) to produce a wider acceptable range on the final answer.

### Penalties & Tiebreakers
- Up to a **10% penalty** for: not returning measuring devices to their original location, not cleaning up spills, or intentionally altering/damaging equipment or objects.
- **Ties are broken using designated tiebreaker stations**, chosen before the event starts.

## Key Competitive Realities
- **This event has three genuinely distinct skill types packed into one event**, and none of them substitute for the others: intuitive/sensory estimation (Section One), fast recall-based unit conversion (Section Two), and precise instrument reading with correct significant-figure/estimated-digit formatting (Section Three). A team strong in one section can still lose significant points in another.
- **The "estimated digit" rule in Section Three is a strict, specific formatting requirement that is easy to get wrong even with an accurate measurement** — the example in the rules explicitly shows that a numerically-close-but-incorrectly-formatted answer (e.g., "9" instead of "9.0") is marked fully wrong, not partially credited. This is a rules-literacy issue as much as a measurement-skill issue.
- **Calculated measurement stations combine measurement accuracy with formula application** — a team needs to both measure precisely AND correctly apply the right formula (density, area, spring constant, etc.) under a tight per-station time limit, compounding two skills into one scored answer.
- **Estimation accuracy is trainable through repeated calibration practice** — since scoring is based on percentage deviation from truth, teams that practice estimating known quantities (and get feedback on how far off they were) can systematically improve their "estimation calibration" for common properties like mass, volume, and length.
- **The event heavily restricts external aids** (no notes, no scratch measuring tools, no extra materials) — meaning success depends entirely on internalized skill (mental math, calibrated intuition, instrument literacy) rather than reference materials, unlike many other Science Olympiad events.
- **Metric conversions are conceptually simple but must be executed flawlessly under a hard 5-minute cap for 5 problems** — since all-or-nothing scoring applies per problem (correct value AND correct unit, or zero), speed and precision in prefix manipulation (kilo-, centi-, milli-, deci-, hecto-, etc.) is the actual bottleneck, not conceptual understanding of what a conversion is.
- **Different tournament levels are meaningfully different competitions**, not just "the same test with a stricter grading curve" — the proportion of calculation-based stations roughly triples from Regionals to Nationals (up to 20% → up to 60%), meaning higher-level prep needs much heavier emphasis on formula-based calculated measurements, not just faster/more precise direct reading.
